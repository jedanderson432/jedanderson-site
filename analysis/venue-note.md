# Venue note

For: *It Has Read Everything We Ever Wrote. It Has Never Read a River.*
Prepared 2026-08-02.

**The honest headline: this piece is not engineered for a masthead to accept it. It is engineered for Figure 2 to spread.** Optimise accordingly, and read §5 first if you only read one section.

Current length: body ~5,050 words plus a ~1,100-word objections-and-falsifiers sidebar. **Every venue below has a word limit lower than that.** Any submission requires a cut, and the cut is the sidebar-to-box move plus roughly 500 words from the body.

---

## 1. General-interest

### The Atlantic—Ideas / Technology
- **Length:** 1,500–4,000 for Ideas; features longer but effectively closed.
- **Unsolicited:** technically accepted via pitch form; in practice **commissioned**. Ideas runs on existing relationships and a stable of contributors.
- **Fit:** strong on the reframe, weak on the technical density. They would want the lede, the denominator, the constitution audit, and almost nothing else.
- **Honest probability for an unsolicited submission from an independent researcher: 2–4%.** Rises to maybe 15% with a warm introduction from a contributing writer.
- **What would move it:** the constitution audit released first as a standalone finding that gets picked up. Atlantic responds to a result that is already circulating.

### Noema
- **Length:** 2,000–5,000. **The only venue on this list whose native length fits.**
- **Unsolicited:** yes, genuinely. `submissions@noemamag.com`. Berggruen-funded, actively seeks unaffiliated thinkers, and publishes long first-principles arguments with technical content intact.
- **Fit:** best on the list. Noema publishes exactly this register—big reframe, physics content permitted, willing to run the objection box.
- **Probability: 12–18%.** The highest realistic number here.
- **What would move it:** pitch the *is/ought split* as the intellectual frame rather than the data-centre reframe. Noema buys ideas, not news pegs.

### Wired—Ideas / Backchannel
- **Length:** Ideas 1,200–2,000; Backchannel features 3,000–6,000 but nearly all commissioned.
- **Unsolicited:** Ideas accepts pitches; Backchannel does not meaningfully.
- **Fit:** Ideas would take the constitution audit as a standalone 1,500-word piece and would probably want a sharper news peg.
- **Probability: 5–8% for Ideas at 1,500 words; under 2% for Backchannel.**
- **Note:** an Ideas placement on the audit alone would be a good outcome—it is the finding most likely to travel and it does not need the rest of the argument.

### MIT Technology Review
- **Length:** 1,000–3,000 for opinion/analysis.
- **Unsolicited:** accepts pitches; opinion section is relatively open.
- **Fit:** good on the EO-model material, cool on the environmental-law material, and they have covered AI energy heavily enough that "actually the footprint is the wrong fight" reads as a contrarian take they may have already run a version of.
- **Probability: 6–10%.**
- **Risk:** their audience already knows the reward-hacking literature, which strips out the part that makes the piece feel new to a general reader.

## 2. Environmental

### Heatmap News
- **Length:** 800–2,500.
- **Unsolicited:** yes, and they are notably open to practitioner voices.
- **Fit:** strong on the energy numbers and the ratepayer material, and they have covered data-centre load closely. **They are also the venue most likely to be hostile to the reframe**, because their readership is the constituency the piece is trying to recruit rather than correct. That is a feature if the editing preserves the concession and a disaster if it does not.
- **Probability: 15–20%**—the highest on the list, at a length that requires losing two thirds of the piece.
- **Recommended cut for Heatmap:** lede + footprint concession + denominator + the ask. Drop the physics, the proxy stack and the ladder entirely.

### Grist
- **Length:** 800–2,000.
- **Unsolicited:** yes.
- **Fit:** moderate. Grist's frame is justice and impact; the piece's frame is instrumentation. The environmental-justice objection (the adjudicated corpus encodes who had lawyers) is in the box and would need to move into the body for this venue.
- **Probability: 8–12%.**

### Yale Environment 360
- **Length:** 1,500–3,000, and they run genuinely analytical pieces.
- **Unsolicited:** yes, editor-reviewed.
- **Fit:** good. E360 publishes practitioner analysis and tolerates technical content better than most environmental outlets. The twenty-seven-years-in-practice byline works here.
- **Probability: 10–15%.**

## 3. Policy

### Issues in Science and Technology (ASU / NAS)
- **Length:** 3,000–5,000. **Second-best length fit on the list.**
- **Unsolicited:** yes, and they explicitly welcome practitioner and independent submissions.
- **Fit:** very good. The ask section, the specification, the falsifiers and the regulatory analysis are exactly their register, and they will not flinch at the objection box.
- **Probability: 12–18%**, comparable to Noema and with a slower cycle.
- **Note:** lower reach, higher durability, and the readership includes people who write rules—which is arguably the correct audience. One red-team reviewer argued the real reader is a regulator and the essay is addressed to a lab. Issues is where that gets fixed.

## 5. The fallback, which is actually the plan

State it plainly: **this is the primary strategy and the venue submissions are the option on top of it.**

1. **Canonical publication on jedanderson.org** at `/essays/it-has-never-read-a-river`, with the technical companion at `/essays/environmental-safety-mode`. Permanent URL, CC-BY-4.0, full fact ledger available. The corpus site is already indexed and AI-readable, which matters more than a masthead for a 25-year findability horizon.
2. **arXiv cs.CY** for the companion—it is the artifact with the reference list, the formal treatment and the specification, and cs.CY is the right category. This gives the work a citable identifier independent of any editor's decision. (Requires endorsement for a first cs.CY submission; budget time for it.)
3. **Figure 2 as the travelling artifact.** This is the actual distribution strategy. The figure was built to be true and self-explanatory with no caption, no article, and no author attached. It carries the whole thesis in one image: two columns, a documented 675:1 ratio, and an explicit unconnected gap. If it is right, it spreads without the essay, and the essay is what people find when they go looking for the source.
4. **The specification propagates separately.** `analysis/ESM-spec.md` is deliberately standalone so it can be forked, implemented or argued with by someone who never reads either essay.

**What "success" means here, honestly.** Not a masthead. Success is: one frontier lab adds an environmental environment to a reward-hacking benchmark; or one agency adds an attribution field to a deviation report; or one permit writer publishes a machine-readable condition. Each of those is a named ask in the piece, each is cheap, and each would be worth more than any placement on this list.

## 6. What would raise every probability on this list

- **Release the constitution audit as a standalone dated result first.** It is checkable in an afternoon, it is the single most surprising fact in the piece, and it is the kind of finding that gets picked up and then makes the longer argument commissionable rather than submitted.
- **Get the false-trip study done.** The specification's biggest gap is that it demands false-trip rates be published and provides no method. A worked example against a year of real monitoring data would convert the ladder from a proposal into an engineering artifact, and would answer the strongest technical objection in the box.
- **Wait for F2 to not fire.** Every quarter that passes without an Earth-observation model entering an autonomous control loop makes the central empirical claim more durable and easier to sell. Every quarter also makes the audit staler. These pull in opposite directions; the audit is the perishable one, so lead with it.
